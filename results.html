Aggregate benchmark comparison

Multi-seed, cross-mechanism comparison across all five fault classes, replacing single-run inspection with averaged, confidence-bounded metrics.

Overview

The individual-run pages linked from the sidebar each show one trajectory graph for one seed. This page instead aggregates 8 independent seed realizations per (fault × target × duration × recovery mechanism) combination - 2,504 individual evaluation runs in total - into means with 95% confidence intervals, and further breaks each result down by fault target (sort agent, press agent, or both) and fault duration (transient or permanent). Six recovery arms are compared throughout: none (no recovery, the control condition), rule_based (oracle reconfiguration, told the ground-truth fault state), rule_based_detect (the same corrective heuristic, but required to detect the fault itself from observable signals), supervisor (a trained hierarchical supervisor policy), fault_tolerant_marl (agents trained under domain-randomized faults), and llm_replanning (an LLM agent that reasons over system state and issues a recovery plan).

Note on rule_based vs. the "Rule-based reconfiguration" pages elsewhere on this site: the individual trajectory pages under each fault type currently show the oracle variant only. rule_based_detect is the same corrective action set (reconfigure the affected channel using a fixed heuristic) gated behind self-detection instead of ground truth, and is included here as a distinct arm because detection quality turns out to matter a great deal - see the metric glossary and per-fault sections below.

Why multi-seed

A single evaluation run answers "what happened in this one random draw," not "how does this mechanism perform in general." Two failure modes make a single run actively misleading here: a fault instance can be too mild to produce any measurable effect (a null result - the run looks identical to a fault-free control episode within normal noise), and a full-override oracle mechanism can produce byte-identical outcomes across unrelated fault types purely because it discards the fault's effect entirely once it engages. Averaging 8 independently-seeded realizations per combination - each with its own episode dynamics and its own fault-injection timing - turns these single-draw artifacts into an honest signal, the same reason a hardware benchmark reports an average over many passes rather than one frame's timing.

An earlier version of this evaluation collapsed four weighted penalty terms into a single 0-100 benchmark_score. It was dropped: the weights (safety 0.40 / deficit 0.25 / relapse 0.20 / time-to-recovery 0.15) were a fixed, non-empirical choice, the composite saturated at 100 on a large share of runs (destroying discriminative power exactly where it mattered), and blending safety and speed into one number hid the speed-vs-safety trade-off the research question is actually about. The metrics below are reported as separate, directly interpretable quantities instead.

How to read these charts

Every chart is a grid of bar plots: rows are the fault's target (sort agent / press agent / both), columns are duration (transient / permanent). Bars within a cell are the six recovery arms, in the same left-to-right order every time (none, rule_based, rule_based_detect, supervisor, fault_tolerant_marl, llm_replanning), and the black whisker on each bar is a 95% confidence interval computed across the 8 seed realizations. A missing bar means no data for that arm in that cell (for example, none and fault_tolerant_marl have no fault detector, so they never appear on the detection-quality charts).

Direction is not the same for every metric. Four of the six metrics are "amount of damage" quantities where a shorter bar is the good outcome. Two - fraction of time spent healthy, and detection recall - are the opposite: a taller bar is good. Every chart states its direction explicitly in its title. Each subplot also has its own y-axis, auto-scaled to that panel's data, so bar heights are only comparable within a panel - always check the axis numbers before comparing across rows or columns.

Metric glossary

MetricDirectionDefinition
Corrected degradation-areaLower is betterTotal performance loss during the fault-active window, with the natural non-stationary-reward pattern of a matched no-fault control episode subtracted out. A run whose apparent loss is no larger than the control's own noise floor is flagged as a likely null result rather than credited as a strong recovery.
Weighted safety-violation excessLower is better5×catastrophic + 2×severe + 1×near-miss container-safety events during the fault window, minus the same weighted count from the matched no-fault control episode, floored at zero. Isolates safety violations actually attributable to the fault (or to a mechanism's own intervention), rather than the routine near-capacity operation every run exhibits by design.
Fraction of window spent healthyHigher is betterProportion of the fault-active window in which performance was at or above a recovered threshold. Computed for every event, including null results (correctly close to 1.0 for those), so - unlike time-to-recovery - it does not depend on conditioning on "recovered and not null," and carries no sample-composition bias between mechanisms.
Relapse countLower is betterNumber of times an event's performance dropped back out of the healthy band after first recovering. Flags mechanisms that look acceptable on average but are unstable - recovering, relapsing, and recovering again.
False-recovery rateLower is betterFraction of steps a self-detecting mechanism flagged a fault when none was genuinely active (the false-positive side of detection quality). rule_based is an oracle rather than a detector and is included here at its true value of 0.0, not blank; none and fault_tolerant_marl run no detector at all and have no value.
Intervention recallHigher is betterOf the steps where the fault was genuinely active, the fraction a self-detecting mechanism actually flagged (the false-negative side of detection quality, complementing false-recovery rate). rule_based is trivially 1.0 by construction.

A time-to-recovery metric was evaluated and dropped from this comparison. It is only meaningful over events that both recovered and were not a null result, and that qualifying subset differs enormously in size and composition between mechanisms - a mechanism that fully neutralizes a fault has that event excluded as a null result altogether, leaving only its hardest, unresolved cases to average over, while a mechanism that never touches the fault keeps a larger, easier-composed pool. This produced misleading "faster recovery" numbers for mechanisms doing objectively less. Fraction-of-window-healthy above is reported instead, since it is computed identically for every event regardless of outcome.

Sensor noise

Additive noise on observation vectors - the mildest fault class in the suite. Null-result rates run 31-44% across arms, meaning a large share of individual instances at the default noise level (σ=0.1) are not reliably distinguishable from ordinary episode noise. Read the confidence intervals carefully here in particular.

Findings. This fault only degrades the press channel's forward-looking signal about the sort stage - the press agent's own local sensing stays accurate - making it the mildest, most partial-information fault in the suite. That is consistent with fault_tolerant_marl's domain-randomized training transferring well here while it does not for the near-total failures (agent dropout, Byzantine): the degradation is small enough that a policy trained for general robustness has an easier job than a policy trying to substitute for a fully broken or adversarial teammate. It is also the one fault class where an active mechanism (supervisor) is clearly worse than doing nothing on both headline metrics - see cross-fault conclusions.

Cross-fault conclusions

Doing nothing is safe only against the mildest faults

none and fault_tolerant_marl are statistically indistinguishable from each other on both degradation-area and safety-violation excess for actuator degradation, agent dropout, and Byzantine - and both sit roughly an order of magnitude worse on safety than any actively-correcting mechanism for agent dropout (~17x) and Byzantine (~13x). For faults that fully compromise an agent's decision-making, leaving it uncorrected is measurably dangerous, not just measurably suboptimal - this is the strongest, most defensible result in the suite. The one exception is communication loss, the mildest and most partial fault tested, where fault_tolerant_marl outright wins.

The oracle's outcome does not depend on the fault type

rule_based reports near-identical degradation-area, safety-excess, and healthy-fraction across sensor noise, actuator degradation, agent dropout, and Byzantine (comms_loss differs because it is structurally a press-only fault). This is not an artifact of the evaluation - it is a direct consequence of the mechanism's design. Once an oracle recovery mechanism engages, it fully overrides the affected channel's decision with a state-following heuristic for the remainder of the fault window; the specific mechanism that corrupted the original decision becomes irrelevant, because that original decision is discarded entirely. What matters to an oracle is only when and where it engages, never what caused the engagement. This is a real, structural property worth stating plainly rather than a coincidence to explain away.

Detection quality is a genuine precision/recall trade-off between mechanisms

The three self-detecting arms sit at different points on the trade-off. supervisor has the highest recall of any real detector on sensor noise (0.75-0.83, vs. 0.14-0.5 for the other two) but also the highest false-recovery rate (~0.57-0.58, vs. ~0.40-0.47) and the worst degradation-area/safety-excess on the fault-target combinations where it over-triggers. rule_based_detect and llm_replanning - which share the same underlying detector - sit closer together, trading lower recall for fewer false alarms. Which end of that trade-off is preferable is fault-dependent: supervisor's high recall helps on Byzantine and agent dropout, where missing the fault is costly, but its high false-positive rate is what makes it the single worst-performing mechanism on communication loss, the mildest fault in the suite, where correction is rarely needed at all.

Pooled numbers hide fault-target structure large enough to change conclusions

Averaging across a fault's target (sort agent / press agent / both) can average together results that differ by 50x or more, as with supervisor under sensor noise: near-zero corrected degradation-area on target=sort, roughly 50x that on target=press/both. A pooled headline number for that cell would read as an unremarkable middling result; the faceted view shows it is actually "irrelevant on one channel, one of the worst arms tested on the other." Any comparison across mechanisms should be read at the target/duration level, not the pooled level, before it is trusted.

What this does and does not establish

Null-result rates ranged from 0% to 59% across (fault, recovery) combinations - a meaningful fraction of individual fault instances, especially for sensor noise, are not statistically distinguishable from ordinary episode noise at the configured injection strength. This is expected and is not filtered out of the reported means; with 8 seeds per cell it washes out rather than dominating the average, but it does mean the confidence intervals shown should be read as indicative rather than as a formal significance test - no paired significance testing across mechanisms has been run yet. The safety-violation-excess and degradation-area results for agent dropout, Byzantine, and actuator degradation are large enough relative to their confidence intervals to be read with confidence; results for the milder sensor-noise and communication-loss classes, and any single target/duration cell with a wide error bar, should be treated as directional rather than conclusive.
